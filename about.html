1
The Goal

Opening a Door That May Not Currently Be Open

I built this to show what I am capable of creating with my current skills in data analysis, AI tooling, and SE process knowledge. The intent is straightforward: I want a hiring manager at Locus to be able to review this, see that I understand SE process and have done serious research on Locus and their customers, and want to have a conversation with me about it.

Most SE applications look the same. A resume, maybe a cover letter, a list of past accounts. I wanted to do something different — something that shows how I actually think through a customer problem, not just that I have done it before. This is my way of standing out and giving someone a concrete reason to pick up the phone.

2
My Background

Where the Warehouse Knowledge Comes From

I have been an SE for approximately five years, with a range of adjacent roles that have expanded my perspective well beyond a typical sales engineering function. The most formative of those was at 6 River Systems, where I had a blast quickly understanding the P2G and R2G product space and the types of customers and applications that go with it. At 6RS specifically, I managed a significant portion of their data offering — work that gave me a clear view into not only how the robots operate, but how customers actually utilize them day to day.

Across those five years I have worked with many different types of AMRs and fixed automation, which has given me an invaluable perspective when analyzing customer sites and operations. I can walk a facility and quickly identify where the real bottlenecks are, which is the starting point for any honest robotics recommendation.

Why Locus

Why I Spent This Much Time on This Specific Company

Since working in the AMR segment, I quickly identified Locus as a leader in the space — and a seemingly great one at that. The growing team, the long employee tenure, and the expanding product portfolio all point to a company on a strong trajectory. That matters to me when choosing where to invest my time and career.

I want to bring my SE experience with AMRs across a variety of customer environments, along with my more recent exposure to AI tooling and data analysis, to what I believe is already a powerful team. The goal is to add efficiency and optimization to a group that clearly already knows what they are doing.

4
How I Built It

The Process, and How I Used AI

I spent a significant amount of time upfront on AI-assisted research into Locus — their products, their customers, their competitors, and how their robots actually operate in the field. I used my domain knowledge to direct that research toward specific things I did not know, and I pressed hard for sources. Any time AI made an assumption or offered an estimate, I challenged it on realism and alignment before letting it go into the model.

The harder part of working with AI is keeping it on the rails. It consistently wanted to build more than was needed. I had to re-enter planning phases multiple times to make sure the end state of this project stayed within my original scope. More time on this project would go toward continuing that refinement — further matching the tooling to Locus' actual offering and to what would set them apart in front of a customer.

How to read the AI involvement here: AI wrote the code. I directed the analysis, validated every output against my domain knowledge, challenged the assumptions, and made every decision about what to build, what to cut, and what the numbers should mean. The analytical framework is mine — the implementation was accelerated.
5
What I Stand Behind

Where the Work Is Solid

I am confident in the calculations. The way this analysis looks at the customer data — the ABC tiering, the walk distance model, the fleet sizing math, the ROI comparison — is sound. I can defend those outputs in a room with a customer's operations team because the methodology is transparent and the logic holds.

What I cannot vouch for are the Locus-specific inputs: the exact pick rates for Origin and Array in a given environment, the precise UPH improvement a customer would see, the actual pricing. Those are planning assumptions, and they are flagged throughout as exactly that.

6
Honest Gaps

What a Real Engagement Would Fix

The weakest parts of this analysis are the assumptions I had to make because the data does not exist outside of a real customer engagement: how far off a customer's slotting strategy actually is, what Locus robots cost under a specific contract, how fast Array picks in a specific item mix. I did not build anything overly complex, so the simple calculations are straightforward — but the inputs they depend on would all need to be validated on-site or with the Locus account team before going in front of a customer with a real number.

The ROI model and fleet sizing math are valid frameworks. The specific outputs — $1.4M net benefit, 4-month payback — are only as reliable as the assumptions underneath them. A real engagement replaces those assumptions with measured data.
7
Where This Goes

What I Would Do With This at Locus

The first thing I would do is replace every Locus-specific assumption in these calculators with real data from the account team — actual pricing, actual pick rates by robot type and item profile, actual UPH improvement ranges from live deployments. That alone would make this a significantly more useful tool.

From there, I would want to understand two things from the internal SE team: what takes the most time and creates the most friction in a typical engagement, and what customers actually care about most when they are evaluating an ROI. Those two inputs would drive where the calculators get refined next. Building tools that do not address those two questions is just building for the sake of building.
